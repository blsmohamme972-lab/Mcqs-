## Cardiovascular history (p2)

## Computed tomography (p16)

1. **Q:** Why is a chest CT important before a cardiac reoperation?  
   **A:** It identifies structures at risk during sternal re-entry: patent grafts, aorta and RV.

   **A:** Chest CT.

   _A must know · p17_

   **A:** To define vascular structures.  
   _B should know · p16_

5. **Q:** List the uses of chest CT in cardiac surgical patients.  
   **A:** Reoperations; coronary assessment in low-risk valve patients; aortic aneurysm and dissection; mediastinitis, sternal dehiscence, PE.  
   _B should know · p16_

6. **Q:** Why must a chest CT be reviewed on lung windows as well as soft tissue windows?  
   **A:** Lung windows show detailed lung parenchyma, which cannot be seen on soft tissue windows.  
   _B should know · p16_

7. **Q:** How accurate is CT angiography for coronary disease?  
   **A:** Very specific and sensitive.  
   _B should know · p16_

8. **Q:** What can and cannot a non-contrast chest CT show about an aortic aneurysm?  
   **A:** Calcification in the aortic wall, but little other information about the vessel lumen.  
   _B should know · p17_

9. **Q:** A valve patient at low risk of coronary disease needs preoperative coronary assessment: what test may replace catheterization?  
   **A:** CT coronary angiography.  
   _B should know · p16_

10. **Q:** Why is CT coronary angiography of little use in patients at higher risk of coronary disease?  
   **A:** Any significant abnormality must then be evaluated with standard coronary angiography anyway.  
   _B should know · p16_

11. **Q:** After sternotomy a patient is suspected of mediastinitis or sternal dehiscence: what imaging helps?  
   **A:** Chest CT, which also evaluates PE.  
   _B should know · p16_

   _C nice to know · p16_

13. **Q:** What is the Hounsfield scale, and what values mark water, white and black?

   _C nice to know · p16_

   **A:** Bone plus 400 to 1000; contrast plus 800; soft tissue plus 40 to 80; lung minus 500.  
   _C nice to know · p16_

   _C nice to know · p16_

   _C nice to know · p17_

## Cardiac catheterization (p18)

1. **Q:** What area reductions correspond to 50%, 70% and 90% diameter stenoses?  
   **A:** 75%, 90% and 99% respectively.  
   _A must know · p19_

2. **Q:** When is a coronary stenosis significant, and why?  
   **A:** When more than 50% of lumen diameter, because it reduces the normal 3 to 4 times flow reserve.  
   _A must know · p19_

3. **Q:** What is the effect of a more than 70% diameter stenosis on coronary flow?  
   **A:** It eliminates any capacity to increase flow above resting flow.  
   _A must know · p19_

4. **Q:** A patient has an acute type A dissection: is cardiac catheterization appropriate?  
   **A:** The patient may be too unwell to tolerate it; otherwise contraindications to catheterization are few.  
   _A must know · p18_

5. **Q:** A patient with aortic stenosis or mobile vegetations is having catheterization: what must be avoided, and why?  
   **A:** Ventriculography and pull-back gradients: crossing the aortic valve risks stroke; echo gives hemodynamics more safely.  
   _A must know · p18_

6. **Q:** The LAD looks completely occluded on one view: what pitfall must you exclude, and how?  
   **A:** A large diagonal mistaken for an occluded LAD: always look at the LAO view before commenting on the LAD.  
   _A must know · p19_

7. **Q:** Which catheterization complications need emergency bypass surgery, and how often?  
   **A:** LAD dissection, acute LAD occlusion and cardiac tamponade; 1 in 1000 catheterizations.  
   _A must know · p18_

8. **Q:** What happens if the puncture site is not occluded after catheter removal?  
   **A:** Extensive hematoma, which may be retroperitoneal if the femoral puncture was high.  
   _A must know · p18_

9. **Q:** What are the access routes for left and right heart catheterization?  
   **A:** Left: femoral or radial artery. Right: femoral, internal jugular or subclavian vein.  
   _B should know · p18_

10. **Q:** What does left heart catheterization measure?  
   **A:** LA, LV and aortic pressures; transmitral and transaortic gradients; mitral and aortic valve areas.  
   _B should know · p18_

11. **Q:** What therapeutic procedures are done by left heart catheterization?  
   **A:** PCI; balloon mitral valvuloplasty; TAVI; LA appendage occlusion.  
   _B should know · p18_

12. **Q:** What does right heart catheterization measure?  
   **A:** RA, RV and PA pressures, PAWP, and saturations including mixed venous saturation (SvO2).  
   _B should know · p18_

13. **Q:** What can be calculated or done at right heart catheterization?  
   **A:** PVR, cardiac output and shunts; pulmonary angiography; transseptal puncture for LA access.  
   _B should know · p18_

14. **Q:** What antiplatelet doses may be given around PCI?  
   **A:** Clopidogrel 300 mg or aspirin 75 mg, before and after PCI.  
   _B should know · p18_

15. **Q:** In which views are the left main stem and the circumflex best seen?  
   **A:** LMS: RAO and LAO caudal. Circumflex: RAO.  
   _B should know · p19_

16. **Q:** In which view are the LAD and diagonals best seen?  
   **A:** The LAO cranial view.  
   _B should know · p19_

17. **Q:** How does ventriculography differ from coronary angiography in contrast use?  
   **A:** Ventriculography requires substantially more contrast.  
   _B should know · p18_

18. **Q:** What general rules apply when reviewing a coronary angiogram?  
   **A:** Look at each lesion in several views and at the whole film; comment on the vessels systematically.  
   _B should know · p19_

19. **Q:** Why must an eccentric plaque be viewed in several projections?  
   **A:** It looks most significant end on and may almost disappear when viewed perpendicularly.  
   _B should know · p19_

20. **Q:** Why may angiography underestimate stenosis in diffusely diseased vessels?  
   **A:** Lesions are judged against adjacent 'normal' segments, which in diffuse disease are themselves diseased.  
   _B should know · p19_

21. **Q:** What TIMI flow does a more than 90% stenosis usually produce?  
   **A:** TIMI grade 1 or 2 flow.  
   _B should know · p19_

22. **Q:** An apparently occluded vessel appears late on injection of another vessel: what is happening?  
   **A:** Retrograde filling: most commonly PDA and RCA from the LAD, sometimes LAD from the PDA.  
   _B should know · p19_

23. **Q:** After the summary, in what order do you describe a coronary angiogram?  
   **A:** LMS; circumflex system; LAD and diagonals; RCA and PDA with dominance; then ventricular function.  
   _B should know · p19_

24. **Q:** Apart from the coronary lesions, what else do you describe on an angiogram?  
   **A:** Ventricular function, any regional akinesia, MR, and calcification of the coronaries or valves.  
   _B should know · p19_

25. **Q:** A patient with renal dysfunction needs catheterization: what should be avoided, and why?  
   **A:** Ventriculography: the increased contrast load significantly worsens renal impairment.  
   _B should know · p18_

26. **Q:** What are the rates of major adverse events and death after cardiac catheterization?  
   **A:** Major adverse events such as stroke 1%; death 0.01%.  
   _B should know · p18_

27. **Q:** How is cardiac catheterization classified?  
   **A:** Right and/or left heart; diagnostic or therapeutic.  
   _C nice to know · p18_

28. **Q:** What is the commonest indication for left heart catheterization?  
   **A:** Coronary angiography.  
   _C nice to know · p18_

29. **Q:** What is right heart catheterization essentially the same as?  
   **A:** Floating a Swan-Ganz catheter.  
   _C nice to know · p18_

30. **Q:** Describe the setup for coronary angiography.  
   **A:** Patient awake but sedated and supine; sheath access by a modified Seldinger technique.  
   _C nice to know · p18_

31. **Q:** How is contrast delivered to the coronary arteries at angiography?  
   **A:** Injected into each coronary ostium in turn via a size 4 Judkins catheter, 2 to 3 mm in diameter.  
   _C nice to know · p18_

32. **Q:** How are angiographic moving images recorded today?  
   **A:** On digital CDs, which have superseded cine film.  
   _C nice to know · p18_

33. **Q:** Which projections are used to image the coronary arteries?  
   **A:** PA, RAO and LAO views, angled cranially or caudally.  
   _C nice to know · p19_

34. **Q:** How are the different angiographic views obtained?  
   **A:** X-ray tube and camera sit opposite each other on a C-arm, rotated cranially, caudally and left to right.  
   _C nice to know · p18_

35. **Q:** What is the advantage of biplane imaging?  
   **A:** Two cameras give simultaneous images from two angles, reducing radiation exposure.  
   _C nice to know · p18_

36. **Q:** How do you open your presentation of a coronary angiogram?  
   **A:** With a summary after viewing all views, e.g. a 75-year-old man with 3-vessel disease and moderate LV.  
   _C nice to know · p19_

## Interpreting coronary imaging (p20)

1. **Q:** Name the branches of the right coronary artery in order.  
   **A:** Conus; SA nodal; RV branch; acute marginal; AV nodal; PDA; LVB or posterolateral branches.  
   _A must know · p21_

2. **Q:** Which artery gives the PDA in right- and left-dominant patients, and how common is each?  
   **A:** Right-dominant (85%): the RCA. Left-dominant (10%): the circumflex.  
   _A must know · p21_

3. **Q:** What branches arise from the LAD and from the circumflex?  
   **A:** LAD: septals at 90 degrees and diagonals. Circumflex: obtuse marginals, AV nodal (40%), and in left dominance PDA and LVB.  
   _A must know · p21_

4. **Q:** How is the LAD divided into proximal, mid and distal segments?  
   **A:** Proximal up to the 1st septal; mid until the 2nd diagonal; distal after the 2nd diagonal.  
   _A must know · p21_

5. **Q:** How often does the AV nodal artery arise from the RCA, and how often from the circumflex?  
   **A:** RCA 60%; circumflex 40%.  
   _B should know · p21_

6. **Q:** How is the RCA divided into proximal, mid and distal segments?  
   **A:** Proximal up to the RV branch; mid up to the acute marginal; distal up to the bifurcation.  
   _B should know · p21_

7. **Q:** How does the left main stem divide?  
   **A:** Into LAD and circumflex; occasionally it trifurcates, with a ramus intermediate branch.  
   _B should know · p21_

8. **Q:** What systematic approach avoids common mistakes when reading coronary angiograms?  
   **A:** Identify each view; look at all views; each vessel in multiple views; the whole clip, as some vessels fill late.  
   _B should know · p20_

9. **Q:** How do you identify the circumflex on any angiographic view?  
   **A:** It is the vessel closest to the spine, or to the catheter in the descending aorta: the most posterior vessel.  
   _B should know · p20_

10. **Q:** How do you identify the LAD on an angiogram?  
   **A:** It travels towards the apex, is usually the longest vessel, and gives septals at 90 degrees.  
   _B should know · p20_

11. **Q:** What branches does the posterior descending artery give, and at what angle?  
   **A:** Inferior septals, coming off at 90 degrees.  
   _C nice to know · p21_

12. **Q:** What are the five common angiographic views?  
   **A:** RAO and LAO of the right coronary; RAO, LAO and LAO caudal ('spider view') of the left coronary system.  
   _C nice to know · p21_

13. **Q:** How do you tell a RAO from a LAO view?  
   **A:** RAO: ribs go down to the right, image right of spine. LAO: ribs down to the left, image left of spine.  
   _C nice to know · p20_

14. **Q:** How do you tell a cranial from a caudal angiographic view?  
   **A:** Cranial: the diaphragm is superimposed over most of the picture. Caudal: it is hardly seen.  
   _C nice to know · p20_

15. **Q:** On a RAO view of the right coronary, how do you distinguish the PDA from the LVB?  
   **A:** The PDA gives off short septals at 90 degrees.  
   _C nice to know · p20_

## Cardiac hemodynamics (p22)

1. **Q:** How is pulmonary vascular resistance calculated, and what is normal?  
   **A:** (Mean PAP minus mean LAP) over cardiac output; PAWP replaces LAP without pulmonary disease; normal 3 Wood units.  
   _A must know · p23_

2. **Q:** A borderline stenosis is assessed with FFR: what value means the lesion is significant?  
   **A:** Less than 75%, i.e. less than 75% of maximal flow in a non-diseased coronary.  
   _A must know · p22_

3. **Q:** Pulmonary hypertension is found at catheterization: how do you decide whether it is fixed?  
   **A:** Fixed pulmonary hypertension cannot be reversed with NTG, inhaled NO, Flolan or exercise.  
   _A must know · p23_

4. **Q:** Classify coronary flow by the TIMI scale.  
   **A:** 0 no antegrade flow; 1 penetration, incomplete distal filling; 2 delayed but complete filling; 3 normal flow.  
   _B should know · p22_

5. **Q:** What methods can measure cardiac output during catheterization?  
   **A:** Thermodilution, the Fick principle and angiographic methods; all have potential errors.  
   _B should know · p23_

6. **Q:** What is fractional flow reserve?  
   **A:** Essentially the ratio between blood pressure proximal and distal to a lesion, measured at maximal flow.  
   _B should know · p22_

7. **Q:** How is maximal flow induced for FFR, and what does the test therefore assess?  
   **A:** Adenosine or papaverine, normally 4 to 5 times resting flow; so it assesses coronary reserve.  
   _B should know · p22_

8. **Q:** Angiography shows delayed but complete distal filling: what TIMI grade?  
   **A:** TIMI 2, partial reperfusion.  
   _B should know · p22_

9. **Q:** Contrast penetrates a lesion but fills the distal bed incompletely: what TIMI grade?  
   **A:** TIMI 1, penetration without perfusion.  
   _B should know · p22_

10. **Q:** Which aortic valve gradient is the most physiologic?  
   **A:** The mean gradient.  
   _B should know · p22_

11. **Q:** How is valve area calculated at catheterization?  
   **A:** Gorlin equation: area equals flow over velocity, or cardiac output over the square root of the gradient.  
   _B should know · p22_

12. **Q:** Describe the Grossman grading of regurgitation.  
   **A:** I faint, clears in one beat; II proximal less than distal; III equal; IV dense, proximal greater than distal.  
   _B should know · p22_

13. **Q:** State the Fick principle.  
   **A:** Uptake or release of a substance, usually VO2, equals the arteriovenous concentration gradient times cardiac output.  
   _B should know · p23_

14. **Q:** Which two conditions can cardiac catheterization help differentiate?  
   **A:** Restrictive cardiomyopathy and constrictive pericarditis.  
   _B should know · p23_

15. **Q:** How are intracardiac shunts detected and quantified at catheterization?  
   **A:** Oximetry sampling from IVC, SVC, multiple RA sites, PA, coronary sinus and artery.  
   _B should know · p23_

16. **Q:** How is Qp:Qs calculated?  
   **A:** (SaO2 minus MVO2) divided by (PVO2 minus PAO2).  
   _B should know · p23_

17. **Q:** Stenosis severity looks uncertain on angiography: what further assessment helps?  
   **A:** Pressure measurements such as fractional flow reserve (FFR), and intravascular ultrasound (IVUS).  
   _B should know · p22_

18. **Q:** Echocardiography is equivocal: what can cardiac catheterization add?  
   **A:** Severity of valve lesions, pulmonary hypertension, ventricular function and intracardiac shunts.  
   _B should know · p22_

19. **Q:** How would you measure the transaortic valve gradient at catheterization?  
   **A:** LV pressure with simultaneous aortic pressure distal to the valve, or by pulling the catheter back into the aorta.  
   _B should know · p22_

20. **Q:** How would you measure the mitral valve gradient at catheterization?  
   **A:** Simultaneous PAWP or LA pressure (by transseptal puncture) and LV end-diastolic pressure.  
   _B should know · p22_

21. **Q:** Valve area must be estimated in a low cardiac output state: what is the problem, and what helps?  
   **A:** Cardiac output estimation is particularly open to error; dobutamine stress testing is a diagnostic adjunct.  
   _B should know · p22_

22. **Q:** In a low output state, which method of measuring cardiac output is more accurate?  
   **A:** The Fick method, more accurate than thermodilution.  
   _B should know · p23_

23. **Q:** A patient with MR or AI, or on supplemental oxygen, needs cardiac output measured: can you use Fick?  
   **A:** No: the Fick method should not be used in MR, AI, or patients receiving supplemental oxygen.  
   _B should know · p23_

24. **Q:** What does evidence suggest for lesions with an FFR above the significance threshold?  
   **A:** Some data suggest it is safe to watch rather than intervene.  
   _B should know · p22_

25. **Q:** What are the sources of error in thermodilution cardiac output?  
   **A:** Tricuspid regurgitation, incorrect injection technique, and low cardiac output.  
   _B should know · p23_

26. **Q:** Describe the thermodilution technique of measuring cardiac output.  
   **A:** Saline bolus into proximal port, measured at a distal port; equation uses specific gravity and temperatures of fluid and blood.  
   _C nice to know · p23_

27. **Q:** What oxygen consumption is assumed in most labs for the Fick method?  
   **A:** 125 mL/m2; VO2 is usually not measured.  
   _C nice to know · p23_

28. **Q:** How is cardiac output measured angiographically?  
   **A:** Trace the LV end-diastolic and end-systolic images and convert them to volume by modelling.  
   _C nice to know · p23_

29. **Q:** Opacification of the proximal chamber equals the distal chamber: what Grossman grade of regurgitation?  
   **A:** Grade III.  
   _C nice to know · p22_

30. **Q:** On simultaneous LV and aortic traces, how do the peak instantaneous, peak-to-peak and mean gradients compare?  
   **A:** They differ: in the book's example peak instantaneous 100, peak-to-peak 50 and mean 60 mmHg.  
   _C nice to know · p23_

31. **Q:** On a catheter pressure trace, which traces define the mean transmitral gradient?  
   **A:** The LA and LV pressure traces.  
   _C nice to know · p23_

## Echocardiography (p24)

1. **Q:** State the Bernoulli equation for a transvalvular gradient.  
   **A:** Gradient in mmHg equals 4 times vm squared, vm being the mean blood velocity in m/s.  
   _A must know · p24_

2. **Q:** A segment contracts normally at rest but its motion falls during stress. What does this suggest?  
   **A:** Flow-limiting coronary stenosis.  
   _A must know · p25_

3. **Q:** A segment impaired at rest improves on low-dose dobutamine. What does it contain?  
   **A:** Stunned or hibernating myocardium.  
   _A must know · p25_

4. **Q:** Critical aortic stenosis with only a moderate gradient: the gradient rises on stress echo. What does that tell you?  
   **A:** The myocardium will respond well to aortic valve replacement.  
   _A must know · p25_

5. **Q:** What is echocardiography, and what are its two main approaches?  
   **A:** Ultrasound used to assess cardiac conditions; transthoracic (TTE) and transesophageal (TEE).  
   _B should know · p24_

6. **Q:** What does Doppler echo measure, and what does it let you calculate?  
   **A:** Flow velocities from Doppler frequency shifts, hence gradients and valve areas; only moving targets are displayed.  
   _B should know · p24_

7. **Q:** What is colour flow imaging, and what colour convention is used?  
   **A:** Colour-enhanced duplex Doppler combining M-mode and D-mode with flow imaging; flow away blue, towards red (BART).  
   _B should know · p24_

8. **Q:** When is 3D echo particularly useful?  
   **A:** Viewing the morphology of the mitral valve.  
   _B should know · p24_

9. **Q:** How is valve area calculated by the continuity equation?  
   **A:** Valve area (cm²) equals stroke volume divided by (ejection time times 85 times mean velocity).  
   _B should know · p24_

10. **Q:** What are the normal adult LV end-systolic and end-diastolic diameters on M-mode?  
   **A:** End-systolic 25–41 mm; end-diastolic 35–56 mm.  
   _B should know · p25_

11. **Q:** Give the normal shortening fraction and ejection fraction on M-mode.  
   **A:** Shortening fraction 30–40%; ejection fraction 50–65%.  
   _B should know · p25_

12. **Q:** What are the normal aortic root diameter and aortic cusp separation on echo?  
   **A:** Aortic root 20–37 mm; cusp separation 15–26 mm.  
   _B should know · p25_

13. **Q:** What is stress echocardiography, and what information does it give?  
   **A:** Stressing the myocardium chemically or by exercise to assess myocardial viability and regional perfusion.  
   _B should know · p25_

14. **Q:** What is the main drug in pharmacological stress echocardiography?  
   **A:** Dobutamine, in incremental low- or high-dose protocols.  
   _B should know · p25_

15. **Q:** In aortic stenosis, how is the mean transvalvular gradient estimated from peak velocity?  
   **A:** Mean gradient equals 2.4 times maximum velocity squared.  
   _B should know · p24_

16. **Q:** What is fractional shortening, and how does it differ from ejection fraction?  
   **A:** A crude index of LV systolic function, expressed as a percentage but not to be confused with ejection fraction.  
   _B should know · p25_

17. **Q:** How is LV function assessed on echo, qualitatively and quantitatively?  
   **A:** Qualitative: wall motion scoring, graded normal, mild, moderate or severe impairment; quantitative: fractional shortening, ejection fraction.  
   _B should know · p25_

18. **Q:** What is the limitation of quantitative echo measures of LV function?  
   **A:** Fractional shortening and ejection fraction are still subject to operator variability.  
   _B should know · p25_

19. **Q:** On stress echo a segment shows net outward systolic motion and does not thicken. What does it mean?  
   **A:** The segment is infarcted.  
   _B should know · p25_

20. **Q:** How can ischemia on stress echo cause mitral regurgitation?  
   **A:** Papillary muscle ischemia from an inducible septal or posterior wall motion defect.  
   _B should know · p25_

21. **Q:** Name the echo modalities in common use.  
   **A:** B-mode (2D), M-mode, D-mode (Doppler), colour flow imaging and 3D echo.  
   _C nice to know · p24_

22. **Q:** What is B-mode echo?  
   **A:** Brightness mode, also called 2D or real-time: a 2D grayscale picture of a single plane; echo intensity shown as brightness.  
   _C nice to know · p24_

23. **Q:** What is duplex ultrasound?  
   **A:** Doppler combined with grayscale images, to correlate flow with fixed structures.  
   _C nice to know · p24_

24. **Q:** What ultrasound frequency range is used in echocardiography?  
   **A:** 2–5 MHz; it passes through tissues and is reflected from tissue interfaces.  
   _C nice to know · p24_

25. **Q:** What can be calculated from septal and posterior wall thickness in systole and diastole?  
   **A:** Fractional shortening and LV mass; echo measures cardiac dimensions precisely.  
   _C nice to know · p25_

26. **Q:** What is the normal LV mass on echo?  
   **A:** 60–124 g/m².  
   _C nice to know · p25_

27. **Q:** What are the normal left atrial and right ventricular dimensions on M-mode?  
   **A:** Left atrium 19–40 mm; right ventricle 7–23 mm.  
   _C nice to know · p25_

28. **Q:** What model is used for regional wall motion assessment on echo?  
   **A:** A nine-segment model.  
   _C nice to know · p25_

29. **Q:** Compare M-mode with B-mode echo.  
   **A:** M-mode is more accurate, so used for measurements, but gives only a 1D image of structures against time.  
   _C nice to know · p24_

30. **Q:** How do piezoelectric crystals produce an echo image?  
   **A:** As transducers they generate and detect ultrasound, converting electrical to mechanical energy and back; reflections form the image.  
   _C nice to know · p24_

31. **Q:** Explain the Doppler effect as used in echocardiography.  
   **A:** Frequency rises as the target approaches, falls as it recedes; shift is proportional to speed, shown as sound or colour.  
   _C nice to know · p24_

32. **Q:** Why are particular echo views needed to assess some structures?  
   **A:** The ultrasound frequencies used cannot penetrate air or bone.  
   _C nice to know · p24_

33. **Q:** What are the drawbacks of treadmill exercise protocols for stress echo?  
   **A:** Treadmill protocols such as Bruce give limited time to image the heart, and hyperventilation may make imaging difficult.  
   _C nice to know · p25_

   _A must know · p26_

2. **Q:** Give the quantitative echo thresholds for severe mitral regurgitation.  
   **A:** Central jet more than 40% of LA area, regurgitant fraction more than 50%, ERO more than 40 mm².  
   _A must know · p26_

3. **Q:** Besides jet size, which two echo signs indicate severe mitral regurgitation?  
   **A:** Jet impinging on the LA wall; systolic flow reversal in the pulmonary veins.  
   _A must know · p26_

4. **Q:** You must exclude left atrial appendage thrombus or assess the ascending aorta. TTE or TEE, and why?

   _A must know · p26_

5. **Q:** How is TEE performed, and what are its relative contraindications?

   _B should know · p26_

6. **Q:** On TEE, which structures are most anterior, and what lies next to the aortic valve?  
   **A:** The right ventricle and atrium are most anterior; the aortic valve is adjacent to the anterior mitral leaflet.  
   _B should know · p26_

7. **Q:** How is valvular regurgitation graded on echo?  
   **A:** By a number of qualitative and quantitative variables, such as jet size and regurgitant fraction.  
   _B should know · p26_

8. **Q:** Name six mid-oesophageal TOE views.  
   **A:** Four chamber, two chamber, long axis, mitral commissural, aortic valve short axis, bicaval.  
   _B should know · p27_

10. **Q:** What are the echo criteria for mild aortic regurgitation, and how is moderate defined?

   _B should know · p26_

11. **Q:** What are the echo criteria for mild mitral regurgitation, and how is moderate defined?

   _B should know · p26_

12. **Q:** Which mitral segments does the mid-oesophageal mitral commissural view show?  
   **A:** P3, A2 and P1, with the LA, LV and coronary sinus.  
   _B should know · p27_

13. **Q:** A patient with an esophageal stricture needs a TEE. How would you proceed?  
   **A:** A stricture is a relative contraindication; if TEE is done, use a pediatric probe.  
   _B should know · p26_

14. **Q:** What happens if the Doppler beam is not aligned parallel to blood flow?  
   **A:** Velocity is underestimated, and hence the transvalvular gradient.

## Perfusion and viability (p28)

1. **Q:** What are the characteristics of hibernating myocardium?  
   **A:** Reduced contractility (hypokinesis, not akinesis); reduced perfusion, mainly on exercise; viable; improves with revascularization.  
   _A must know · p28_

2. **Q:** Stress SPECT is abnormal and rest images are taken. How do you read fixed and reversible defects?  
   **A:** Fixed defects represent infarcts; reversible defects suggest hibernation.  
   _A must know · p28_

3. **Q:** Poor LV function, mainly breathless rather than anginal, considered for CABG. What must you assess?  
   **A:** Myocardial viability (hibernating myocardium): detecting it is clinically important in this group before CABG.  
   _A must know · p28_

4. **Q:** A perfusion defect after stress normalizes at rest. What is it, and what may help?  
   **A:** A reversible defect: reversible ischemia, which may be improved by revascularization.  
   _A must know · p29_

5. **Q:** A photon-deficient area is unchanged after stress and at rest. What is it, and will revascularization help?  
   **A:** A fixed defect, usually infarction, which will not be improved by revascularization.  
   _A must know · p29_

6. **Q:** In poor LV function, what does absence of viability imply for high-risk CABG?  
   **A:** Much lower prognostic and symptomatic benefit from the surgery.  
   _A must know · p28_

7. **Q:** What did the term 'hibernating myocardium' originally describe?  
   **A:** The theory that chronic LV dysfunction was an adaptive response to chronically reduced coronary flow at rest.  
   _B should know · p28_

8. **Q:** What are the key modalities for assessing myocardial viability?  
   **A:** Nuclear imaging, stress echocardiography and MRI.  
   _B should know · p28_

9. **Q:** What tracer is most widely used in cardiac PET, and what does it reflect?  
   **A:** 18F-fluoro-2-deoxyglucose, taken up by myocytes, reflecting glucose metabolism during ischemia.  
   _B should know · p28_

10. **Q:** Which radionuclides are used to image hibernation?  
   **A:** Thallium-201 (thallium chloride) and 18F-FDG.  
   _B should know · p29_

11. **Q:** How does cardiac MRI assess myocardial viability?  
   **A:** End-diastolic wall thickness, dobutamine stress for contractile reserve, and contrast enhancement revealing scar.  
   _B should know · p29_

12. **Q:** What do more recent studies suggest is the mechanism of hibernation?  
   **A:** Repetitive stunning: adequate perfusion at rest, ischemia on exercise from impaired coronary flow reserve.  
   _B should know · p28_

13. **Q:** In hibernating myocardium, what wall motion shows that the muscle is viable?  
   **A:** Hypokinesis rather than akinesis: the muscle is viable, not infarcted.  
   _B should know · p28_

14. **Q:** What is contractile reserve, and what does it predict?  
   **A:** Improvement with inotropes or exercise; it can predict functional improvement after revascularization.  
   _B should know · p28_

15. **Q:** Why does a flow-limiting stenosis cause a defect on stress perfusion imaging?  
   **A:** During stress its territory receives relatively less blood than adjoining normal territories.  
   _B should know · p29_

16. **Q:** A thallium scan shows apical defects after stress that reverse on rest images. Interpret it.  
   **A:** Reversible ischemia in the apical territory.  
   _B should know · p29_

17. **Q:** What does nuclear cardiac imaging allow, and what isotopes are used?  
   **A:** Ventriculography and perfusion studies; gamma-ray or positron-emitting radionuclides.  
   _C nice to know · p28_

18. **Q:** Which technetium compounds are replacing thallium in nuclear perfusion imaging?  
   **A:** Technetium-99m compounds such as sestamibi and tetrofosmin, replacing cheaper thallium-201 compounds.  
   _C nice to know · p28_

19. **Q:** What is SPECT?  
   **A:** Two gamma cameras with computerized reconstruction, so technetium or thallium images can be reconstructed in any plane.  
   _C nice to know · p28_

20. **Q:** Which technetium-99m compounds are used in cardiac nuclear imaging, and for what?  
   **A:** Labeled red cells, MIBI and pyrophosphate: blood pool studies, ventriculography and myocardial perfusion.  
   _C nice to know · p29_

21. **Q:** What are nitrogen-13 and the oxygen radionuclide used to image?  
   **A:** Nitrogen-13: myocardial perfusion; the oxygen radionuclide: oxygen uptake.  
   _C nice to know · p29_

22. **Q:** What does nuclear ventriculography provide?  
   **A:** An accurate, relatively non-invasive, reproducible ejection fraction, and intracardiac shunts.  
   _C nice to know · p29_

23. **Q:** How is myocardial perfusion imaging performed, and with what stressors?  
   **A:** Regional flow is imaged after stress then at rest; stressors adenosine, dobutamine and exercise.  
   _C nice to know · p29_

24. **Q:** Besides viability, what does cardiac MRI measure accurately, and what can CT also show?  
   **A:** LV and RV volumes and ejection fraction; CT can also show late enhancement, wall thickness and volumes.  
   _C nice to know · p29_

25. **Q:** How does conventional radionuclide imaging produce perfusion images?  
   **A:** Tracer taken up by red cells decays to gamma rays, hitting a sodium iodide crystal; a photomultiplier detects scintillations.  
   _C nice to know · p28_

26. **Q:** Compare PET with SPECT for myocardial imaging.  
   **A:** PET has much better spatial resolution than SPECT's 1 cm, but routine use is limited by very high cost.  
   _C nice to know · p28_

27. **Q:** How is a SPECT perfusion study sequenced?  
   **A:** Stress imaging first (exercise, dobutamine, dipyridamole or adenosine); if normal, rest imaging is not needed.  
   _C nice to know · p28_

28. **Q:** What 1970s observation challenged the view that chronic ventricular dysfunction meant infarction?  
   **A:** That chronic ventricular dysfunction could be reversed by revascularization.  
   _C nice to know · p28_

## General pre-op checklist (p30)

1. **Q:** What do risk stratification tools estimate, and which are the main ones?  
   **A:** Operative risk; the main systems are EuroSCORE, the STS score and the Parsonnet score.  
   _A must know · p30_

2. **Q:** Describe EuroSCORE.  
   **A:** Based on European CABG patients; simple additive and more accurate logistic versions; tends to overestimate operative risk.  
   _A must know · p31_

3. **Q:** When do you stop warfarin before cardiac surgery?  
   **A:** 3–5 days preoperatively.  
   _A must know · p31_

4. **Q:** A patient with a mechanical valve on warfarin needs cardiac surgery. How do you manage anticoagulation?  
   **A:** Stop warfarin 3–5 days before; admit early and start heparin, stopped 6 hours before surgery.  
   _A must know · p31_

5. **Q:** When are aspirin and clopidogrel stopped before surgery in low-risk patients?  
   **A:** Aspirin 5 days and clopidogrel 10 days preoperatively.  
   _A must know · p31_

6. **Q:** How do you manage an insulin-dependent diabetic on your list?  
   **A:** First on the list; morning insulin replaced by a sliding scale and iv fluids.  
   _A must know · p31_

7. **Q:** Which patients need a carotid Doppler before cardiac surgery?  
   **A:** Those with a history of CVA, TIA or severe peripheral vascular disease.  
   _A must know · p31_

8. **Q:** When may carotid intervention be indicated before cardiac surgery?  
   **A:** Internal carotid stenosis more than 70%, especially if the other side is tightly stenosed, to reduce CVA risk.  
   _A must know · p31_

9. **Q:** A patient listed for valve surgery has a neutrophilia. What do you do?  
   **A:** Cancel, and find and treat the source of infection.  
   _A must know · p31_

10. **Q:** What is the STS score, and how does it compare with EuroSCORE?  
   **A:** A similar model validated in North American patients, freely available as an online calculator.  
   _B should know · p31_

11. **Q:** What must be available before emergency cardiac surgery?  
   **A:** ICU bed; surgeon; anesthesiology team; operating room; two scrub nurses; perfusionist; blood.  
   _B should know · p30_

12. **Q:** What should you always do with the patients on your elective list?  
   **A:** Always see the patients you operate on: take your own history and do your own examination.  
   _B should know · p30_

13. **Q:** What do you check before an elective cardiac list?  
   **A:** Consent; bloods reviewed and cross-matched; CXR reviewed and films available; any additional investigations needed.  
   _B should know · p30_

14. **Q:** When should a preoperative echo or angiogram be repeated?  
   **A:** If it is more than 6 months old.  
   _B should know · p30_

15. **Q:** Why are sick, complex patients placed first on the list?  
   **A:** They will not tolerate cancellation and benefit from early ICU admission while imaging and support services are fully available.  
   _B should know · p30_

16. **Q:** What arrangements may need to be made in advance for an elective list?  
   **A:** Order homografts; contact pacing technicians for pacemaker patients; notify theatres of latex allergy.  
   _B should know · p30_

17. **Q:** Which patient factors should you tell the perfusionist about?  
   **A:** Anemia, weight less than 60 kg, and high creatinine.  
   _B should know · p30_

18. **Q:** A patient on your list is anemic. What do you tell the perfusionist, and what follows?  
   **A:** They may prime the pump with blood, so you will need to cross-match extra.  
   _B should know · p30_

19. **Q:** A patient weighs under 60 kg. Why tell the perfusionist?  
   **A:** They may minimize the prime volume to avoid hemodilution.  
   _B should know · p30_

20. **Q:** A patient has a high creatinine. Why tell the perfusionist?  
   **A:** The perfusionist can filter the patient on bypass.  
   _B should know · p30_

21. **Q:** When are ACE inhibitors withheld before cardiac surgery?  
   **A:** On the day before surgery.  
   _B should know · p31_

22. **Q:** How do you manage a non-insulin-dependent diabetic before cardiac surgery?  
   **A:** Normal medication, but withhold long-acting hypoglycaemics such as gliclazide on the morning of surgery.  
   _B should know · p31_

23. **Q:** A CABG patient has significant carotid disease. Whom do you involve, and what do you do on bypass?  
   **A:** Discuss with a vascular surgeon; maintain high perfusion pressures.  
   _B should know · p31_

24. **Q:** A patient on your list has a murmur. What do you check?  
   **A:** A recent preoperative TTE, which all patients should have: review it.  
   _B should know · p31_

25. **Q:** A patient has unexplained anemia before elective cardiac surgery. What do you do?  
   **A:** Full outpatient investigation; the surgeon may prime the pump with blood; not normally transfused preoperatively.  
   _B should know · p31_

26. **Q:** A CABG patient has a neutrophilia. When may surgery still go ahead?  
   **A:** If WCC less than 12.0 and CRP normal, after discussion with the surgical team.  
   _B should know · p31_

27. **Q:** How do you prepare a high-risk patient with respiratory impairment for cardiac surgery?  
   **A:** Admit them for physiotherapy, nebulizers and education.  
   _B should know · p31_

28. **Q:** A patient's renal function deteriorates acutely before cardiac surgery. What do you do?  
   **A:** Treat it before surgery: stop nephrotoxic drugs including ACE inhibitors; consider iv fluids.  
   _B should know · p31_

29. **Q:** A patient on your list is MRSA positive. What do you do?  
   **A:** Treat it preoperatively, consulting the infection control team for the appropriate treatment.  
   _B should know · p31_

30. **Q:** A lung lesion is found before CABG. How do you proceed?  
   **A:** Investigate with a lateral film and CT; isolated non-infective lesions do not usually contraindicate coronary surgery.  
   _B should know · p31_

31. **Q:** Besides the operation, what must you specify when writing the list?  
   **A:** Radial artery and side; off-pump; redo; femoral bypass; thoracotomy and side.  
   _C nice to know · p30_

32. **Q:** What do cardiac surgical registries publish?  
   **A:** Institution- and surgeon-specific risk-adjusted mortality and complication rates, allowing for comorbidity, demographics and nature of surgery.  
   _C nice to know · p30_

33. **Q:** What is the Parsonnet score?  
   **A:** One of the earliest risk stratification systems, not widely used now.  
   _C nice to know · p31_

34. **Q:** How is the running order of an elective cardiac list decided?  
   **A:** With the surgeon: sick, complex patients first; infection risks last; equipment availability may dictate order.  
   _C nice to know · p30_

35. **Q:** A patient on your list is an infection risk. Where do you place them?  
   **A:** Last on the list.  
   _C nice to know · p30_

36. **Q:** Who must know the list order, and how?  
   **A:** The anesthesiologist, especially about last-minute changes; nursing staff, with a copy at every patient location, ICU and OR.  
   _C nice to know · p30_

37. **Q:** A patient is an outlier on a ward not used to surgery. What must the nurses know?  
   **A:** Starved from midnight, in a gown, on a stretcher, and shaved at least 30 minutes before send time.  
   _C nice to know · p30_

38. **Q:** What should ICU be told about your list?  
   **A:** Which patients are appropriate for fast-tracking.  
   _C nice to know · p30_

39. **Q:** How are surgical outcome data usually published, and which outliers are highlighted?  
   **A:** Raw index-operation mortality, e.g. isolated CABG, per institution or surgeon; those beyond 2 standard deviations of the risk-adjusted mean.  
   _C nice to know · p31_

40. **Q:** What effect may publishing outcome data have had?  
   **A:** Lower mortality rates, possibly through risk-averse behaviour causing bias against high-risk patients.  
   _C nice to know · p31_
